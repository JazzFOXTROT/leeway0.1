Leeway

Booking a time window for the security check at Ben Gurion Airport, and guiding the traveler through to the end of check-in.

01 · The challenge

The problem isn’t the queue. The problem is that you can’t know when you’ll get out of it

Six of the eight interviewees arrive at Ben Gurion three hours early. That buffer isn’t derived from the load, but from the fact that there’s no way to know what the load will be.

What happens today

There is no tool that shows expected load at Ben Gurion. The Israel Airports Authority app provides only a flight board and parking information.

What the research overturned

I assumed the queue was the bottleneck. One interviewee said the queue had become faster on its own. And still, people arrive three hours early.

The idea

Replace a guessed buffer with a known one: book a time window for the security check in advance, and give it a number you can plan around.

I always get to the airport about three hours before the flight.Real interview 4

02 · User analysis

Two groups, and only one of them really hurts

I grouped the interviewees not by flight frequency but by the quality of their experience so far. Those who experienced disruption plan out of risk avoidance, and those for whom the process ran smoothly focus on efficiency.

Ido Carmeli · primary persona

“It took me more than an hour to get through check-in”

Experienced a queue that blew up. Arrives three hours early every time. His pain isn’t the waiting but the lack of control over what will happen that day.

Michal Avrahami · secondary persona

“In the past year the queue has started to be more orderly”

Manages her timing with an external tool, and has had a smooth process so far. My data shows no distinct pain for her. I flagged that and didn’t invent a second frustration for her.

The persona worked for me when it refused me, not when it agreed

A ready-made onboarding skill suggested an eight-screen skeleton, including a competitor comparison table. I wanted it. Then I thought about Ido: he doesn’t open the app to choose between products, but with one question, what time to be at the airport. A comparison table answers a question he didn’t ask, so it went.

04 · Sitemap

Sitemap: a screen with no stage in the journey doesn’t get in

I cross-checked every stage in the four journey maps against the screens in the Sitemap, in two reviews. Out of them went “FAQ”, “Contact us” and flight history.

The second review also overturned a decision that was already in the design: the registration screen showed two choices, a security window and a check-in window. The duration of screening is unpredictable by nature, so choosing a check-in window at registration is a guess presented as a tool. One real choice remained.

The structure that remained is Status-First: As long as there is no active booking, the main screen is New booking. Once there is one, the app switches automatically to flight-day mode, and returns on its own when check-in ends.

05 · Design language

What I removed explains the language more than what I kept

If the product were a place, it would be a small information desk at the entrance to the queue. One that tells you where you’re standing right now, and doesn’t pretend to be the guard itself.

Certain

The confirmation and QR open even without a live internet connection.

A backup, not a replacement

The screen is meant to be checked alongside a passport and boarding pass, not instead of them.

Upfront about the unknown

The check-in screen doesn’t claim to predict load, because that’s exactly what remains unsolved.

Doesn’t assume everything’s fine

No current data? The last status is shown with a timestamp.

First rejection: a whole direction

I developed four directions, and one of them was warm and friendly. It looked good, but Ido’s emotional intensity at screening drops to 1.5 out of 5. A friendly tone at that moment doesn’t fit its severity. I dropped it.

Second rejection: density

The first round returned a scrolling list of all hours of the day, about thirty rows, and a status screen that was a dashboard. Most of the day is ruled out anyway by the takeoff time, so the list became nine windows in a three-by-three arrangement.

07 · From design to code

Where the model went wrong, and what that taught me

The app was built as HTML, CSS and JavaScript only, from a Figma file that was a rough reference and not a spec. That explains both what worked and what broke.

What worked

Turning a scattered research package into consistent documents, and building the persona, empathy, journey and Sitemap boards directly in the file.

What broke

I asked that the time-selection screen match the design. The nine windows were copied from the frame in Figma and frozen in code, values that were only there as an example.

How I caught it

Only when I added a real flight search. For a flight taking off at 21:10, the screen offered a security window at 6:40 in the morning.

In a design file, a value that’s an example looks exactly like a value that’s a rule, and nobody marks which is which. Telling them apart is my job, not the model’s.

The prompt that changed the result most wasn’t an instruction, but a question

If the check-in window depends entirely on the duration of the security screening, which is unpredictable by nature, why present it at all as a choice at registration?From the prompt history, step 4

That question erased an entire selector from the registration screen. The phrasings before it asked to improve the screen. It asked to justify it.

The three mistakes that broke in code and what the root cause was
What happenedRoot causeHow it was solved

Before and after Three changes that came from people, not from me

I had no measurement tools that worked, so I gave the app to up to seven people and watched.

Before

They didn’t understand what was expected of them

A person opened it, read, looked at the buttons, and then asked out loud: what am I supposed to do here, and what am I actually booking.

After: A three-step walkthrough, with a visual highlight on the relevant element. The screen was readable, the task wasn’t.

Before

Error screen on an empty field

You didn’t enter a flight number, pressed Next, and the system threw you to a full error screen. The user froze.

After: Validation on the same screen, pointing at the missing field. The error screen remains for real edge cases.

Before

Hebrew only

Some of the people who tried it don’t read Hebrew, and some don’t live in Israel. For them the product simply wasn’t usable.

After: Four languages, with a language switcher at the bottom of every screen. The change that had the most impact, more than dark mode.

Three things I learned

  • A demo is not a fact. I caught this twice in the same project, in two different places.
  • Form suggests, even when I didn’t mean to suggest. I built a scrolling area for adding travelers, and the user read the empty fields as an invitation to fill them.
  • A flag isn’t evidence, and you can still decide by it. The passport field was born from a flag I marked as non-evidence, because the cost of error isn’t symmetric.

The next version

  • Predicted load level at check-in. The gap that left the primary persona’s acceptance test partially failed.
  • Last-minute update or postponement of the window. Exists in the wireframes and wasn’t built. Today you can only cancel entirely.
  • Scanning the boarding pass instead of typing. Shortens the form to a single action.

What was actually built

  • A live app, mobile, twelve screens, in four languages and two display modes.
  • A design system in Figma with tokens for two modes, that passed an eleven-point audit including AA contrast.
  • A research package: eight analyzed interviews, two personas, two empathy maps, four journey maps and a Sitemap that went through two reviews.
The full research Eight interviews, four of them real

I interviewed four people, and in parallel built four simulated test scenarios to cover assumptions I had no one to ask about: a frequent business traveler, a parent of small children, a retiree who avoids new apps, and an operations worker.

The simulated interviews are marked as such in every document, they weren’t used to group people into personas, and no quote in the spec was taken from them. They’re flags to check, not data.

01

People arrive early regardless of load

Six of eight described a fixed arrival of two and a half to three and a half hours ahead.

What this changed: The time-window grid is built backward from takeoff time.

02

The bottleneck shifts

Five of eight. For the same person it alternates between flights.

What this changed: Registration books only security. Check-in became a continuously updated estimate.

03

Identified by documents

Four of eight, three of them real interviews: at the station they ask for a passport and a boarding pass.

What this changed: The QR was defined as a digital backup alongside the documents, not in place of them.

The insight that most threatened the project is the one I left in. Interviewee 2 said the queue had become more orderly and faster on its own. If the problem were the length of the queue, the product would be pointless. It isn’t, so the product isn’t.

The gaps I flagged for myself What here isn’t evidence, and why it stayed anyway
  • Two more insights that came up, about unclear identification and about abandoning digital processes, come from a simulated interview only and aren’t counted as evidence.
  • There is no quantitative measurement here. I installed analytics tools and they didn’t reflect the visits I saw with my own eyes. Up to seven people tried it, with no recording and no defined tasks. This is observation, not an experiment.
  • The prompt-history file was written retroactively and reconstructed from the outputs, so the phrasings are a faithful reconstruction and not a word-for-word quote. What’s accurate: which steps ran, in what order, and what came out of them.
