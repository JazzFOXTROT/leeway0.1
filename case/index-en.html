Nobody knows how long the airport will take

At Ben Gurion, departing travelers pass a short security interview at the terminal entrance, before check-in. Its length is hard to predict, so most people simply arrive three hours early. Leeway replaces that guess with a booked window.

“I always get to the airport about three hours before the flight.”Interviewee 4 (translated from Hebrew)

    03 Designing Leeway

    Three decisions shape the product

    One booking choice, a home screen that follows the trip, and a confirmation that works under pressure.

    A · Book

    One clear booking decision

    Early wireframes asked for a security window and a check-in window. I removed the second: after an unpredictable screening, a check-in slot would be a guess dressed as a reservation.

    The screen now offers nine 20-minute windows built from the departure time. The last ends two hours before take-off.

    B · Track

    A status-first home screen

    The home screen keeps one layout, but its status card changes with the traveler’s state, so the next useful action is always the obvious one.

    Designed, not in the prototype

    New booking→Flight day→Back to new booking

    C · Access

    A confirmation that works under pressure

    At the desk, the QR opens full screen in one tap, and the app shell is cached so it loads without a signal.

    Many travelers don’t read Hebrew, so the interface runs in Hebrew, English, Arabic and Russian, with right-to-left layouts where needed. The language switch sits on the home screen, where someone who can’t read the interface will find it.

    Visual language

    Calm and practical, for time-sensitive moments

    • One primary action per screenOnly the windows that fit the flight.
    • Status in plain words“In progress”, “Waiting”, “Completed”.
    • Restrained colorOne blue, red only for problems; light and dark tokens at AA contrast.

    Of four explored directions, I set aside the most playful: a cheerful tone didn’t fit the journey’s most stressful moment.

      05 Outcomes & Reflection

      What changed after people used it

      I watched up to seven people try the prototype, informally. Their reactions led to these changes.

      Errors that explain themselves

      An empty field used to open a full error screen, and people froze. Now the form marks what’s missing in place.

      After: the fields to fix are marked in place.

      From a full day to nine windows

      The first round offered a date strip and windows across the morning. The final grid shows only the nine that fit the flight.

      After: windows derived from the departure time.

      A three-step walkthrough

      People asked what they were booking. A first-visit walkthrough now points at the real controls.

      Four languages

      Several testers didn’t read Hebrew. This had the largest effect of any change.

      International passports

      The field now accepts non-Israeli passport formats.

      Scheduling logic

      The first build froze Figma’s example windows; a 21:10 flight got a 6:40 slot. The rule now lives in one place in code.

      What worked

      One bookable stage kept the flow short and made it easy to cut screens.

      What I learned

      A value in a design file can be an example or a rule. I now mark which before anything is built.

      Next steps

      • Task-based usability tests with recordings
      • More interviews, including check-in-heavy travelers
      • Changing a booked window (wireframed, not built)
      • Scanning the boarding pass instead of typing

      Research methodology 4 real interviews, 4 simulated scenarios

      I interviewed four people. In parallel, I wrote four simulated interviews to test assumptions I had no one to ask about: a frequent business traveler, a parent of small children, a retiree who avoids new apps, and an airport operations worker. They are labeled as simulated in every document, were not used to form the personas, and are not quoted as evidence.

      The passport field came from the first hypothesis. I added it anyway because the costs weren’t symmetric: one more field against a traveler who can’t be identified at the desk. It is also consistent with the real finding that the passport is the first document staff ask for.

      Testing was informal observation of up to seven people, with no recordings and no defined tasks. Analytics were installed but did not record visits reliably, so there is no quantitative data.

      One real interviewee said the line had become faster on its own over the past year. People still arrived three hours early, which pointed to uncertainty, rather than line length, as the problem.

      Personas and journey maps Original FigJam boards
      Information architecture Sitemap after two reviews

      I checked every journey-map stage against the sitemap twice. Screens with no stage in the journey (FAQ, Contact us, flight history) were removed, and so was the check-in window at registration. The final structure has two states with automatic transitions between them. The primary persona’s acceptance test is marked on the board as partially met: the app can’t yet predict check-in load.

      Implementation notes How the prototype was built

      The prototype is plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript, hosted on GitHub Pages. I built it with an AI coding assistant, working from my Figma file and reviewing the result screen by screen. The flight lookup uses keyless public sources: the Israel Airports Authority’s open departures feed for date, time and terminal, and a route database as a fallback. No name or passport number leaves the device.

      BehaviorFirst buildRefined
      Time windowsExample values copied from the Figma frameNine 20-minute windows built backward from departure, defined in one place
      Unknown flightFell back to the demo routeShows the number actually typed, with a neutral card
      Extra passengersCould be added but not removedRemove action with renumbering

      The question that most changed the design came during the build: if the check-in window depends entirely on an unpredictable screening, why offer it as a choice at all? It removed a whole selector from registration.

      Known limitations Scope of the concept
      • No integration with the airport: availability, bookings and stage progress are simulated.
      • The status screen doesn’t predict check-in load. This is the main open question for the primary persona.
      • The return to “new booking” after check-in is defined in the IA but not implemented in the prototype.
      • Evidence is qualitative: four interviews and informal observation.
